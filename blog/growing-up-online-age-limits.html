Growing Up Online, Safely: Why the New Age Limits Are Good News for Families

From Australia to France and Türkiye, countries are setting minimum ages for social media. Here is what is changing, why it is a positive step, and how families and game makers can make it work.

We do not hand car keys to a ten-year-old, and nobody calls that "anti-car". We wait until they are ready, we teach them, and we build roads with seatbelts, crossings and speed limits. In 2026, more and more countries have started to treat the online world the same way. As a studio that makes games for the whole family, we think that is good news — and we want to explain why.

What is changing around the world

In just over a year, minimum ages for social media went from a debate to real rules:

  • Australia went first. Since 10 December 2025, major social media platforms must keep children under 16 off their services.
  • France approved a nationwide ban on social media for children under 15 in July 2026, and extended phone limits in schools.
  • Türkiye passed a law in 2026, and its regulation took effect on 1 November 2026. Social networks may no longer serve children under 15, users aged 15 to 17 get a separate, age-appropriate service, and existing accounts must be age-verified by 31 March 2027. Game platforms are also asked to provide age ratings and parental controls.
  • The United Kingdom announced an under-16 restriction in June 2026, with the detailed rules expected to apply from 2027.
  • The European Union has set out plans to keep children under 13 off social media and to limit access for older children, while countries such as Denmark, Greece, Spain and Norway are preparing their own rules. The UAE, Malaysia and Indonesia have taken similar steps.

The details differ from country to country, but the message is the same: childhood deserves some protected space.

Why this is a positive step

Age limits are not about keeping children away from technology. They are about timing. A few extra years make a real difference:

  • Sleep and focus. Endless feeds and late-night notifications compete with the sleep and concentration that growing brains need. Lawmakers in many countries point to research linking heavy social media use with poorer sleep, shorter attention spans and anxiety in teenagers.
  • Fewer strangers. Open profiles and direct messages put children in contact with people they would never meet in real life. A minimum age closes that door until they are better prepared.
  • Less comparison pressure. Likes, follower counts and filtered photos are hard on anyone — and hardest on a twelve-year-old who is still working out who they are.
  • Shared responsibility. Until now, parents were expected to police huge global platforms alone. The new rules ask the platforms to do their part: verify age, offer parental controls and design safer services.

What it does not mean

It does not mean children lose the internet. Learning apps, encyclopedias, video calls with grandparents, creative tools and age-appropriate games are all still there. In fact, the new rules make it easier to say yes to the good parts, because families can see clear age ratings and use real parental controls.

It is also fair to say that the rules raise honest questions. Age verification has to work without collecting more personal data than necessary, and some teenagers will try to get around any limit. Those concerns are real — and they are a reason to build age checks carefully and privately, not a reason to give up on protecting children.

Five simple habits for families

  1. Talk before you set rules. Ask what your children enjoy online and what worries them. Rules that are explained are rules that are kept.
  2. Make a family media plan. Agree on screen-free times — dinner, homework, the hour before bed — and on where devices sleep at night (not in the bedroom).
  3. Check the age rating. Every app and game on Google Play shows an official age rating. Read it, and look at what the app asks permission for.
  4. Use the tools that exist. Phones and tablets have built-in parental controls and family accounts for screen time, purchases and content filters. Set them up together.
  5. Play together. Ten minutes of playing your child's favourite game with them teaches you more than any article — and it is fun. Children copy what they see, so our own screen habits matter too.

And for young people

If you are under the age limit, you are not missing out — you are getting a head start. Use these years to build things: draw, code, read, make music, play games with friends you know in real life. The online world will still be there when you are ready, and you will arrive with better skills and a stronger sense of who you are.

What we do as a game studio

A kinder internet is a shared project

Laws set the floor. Families, schools and the companies that build the digital world decide how high we go above it. If we get this right, the next generation will grow up with an internet that respects their time, their privacy and their childhood — and that is something worth building together.
